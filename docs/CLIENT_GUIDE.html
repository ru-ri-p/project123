The one idea. Every AI output your product generates passes through a single check before a user sees it. Attest judges it against your own policy and the rulebooks that apply to you, records both the decision and the output as a signed, tamper-evident event, and hands your code a verdict. When something is wrong, Attest also proposes the fix — and, when you allow it, applies it. The promise a regulator hears: “we flagged it, here is the fix, here is proof the fix shipped, and nobody can doctor that history.”

01 — The gate

One call, before the output ships

The whole integration is a single function. After your model produces something, you pass it to gate(). Attest evaluates and records it, then returns a verdict your code reads and acts on. Attest never changes your application's behaviour on its own — you decide what to do with the verdict.

# after your model produces a market brief, before a user sees it
result = attest.gate({"output": brief_text})

if result.blocked:
    serve("This response is being reviewed before release.")
else:
    serve(brief_text)
In practice

Your morning-brief generator drafts a note on gold ahead of the Fed minutes. One gate() call later you know whether it's clean, needs a look, or must be held — and either way there's now a signed record that the check happened, before any client opened the app.

02 — Risk tiers

Four levels, and only your policy can block

Every verdict lands on a four-colour scale. The tier drives what your code does and where the item shows up for a human. A crucial rule: the jurisdiction rulebooks Attest publishes are advisory — they can raise the tier and cite their instrument, but only your own policy can actually block an action. Nothing Attest hasn't had reviewed can stop your business.

Green
Clean. Recorded and served, nothing raised.
Yellow
Minor. Noted; usually served, often auto-fixable.
Orange
Serious. Routed to a person, or auto-fixed if you allow it.
Red
Highest. Held for human clearance by default.
In practice

A brief that mentions a client's email address trips an orange flag for personal data, citing DIFC Data Protection. A brief that reads like individualised investment advice is orange too. An automated instruction to execute a trade — something your own policy forbids the AI from doing unattended — is red and blocked.

03 — Fixes & verified rewrites

Not just a flag — the fix, checked before it's offered

When an output is flagged, the verdict carries a suggested fix. For mechanical problems this is deterministic: personal data redacted to labelled tokens, guaranteed-return language softened, each edit naming the finding it cures. For judgement calls a rule can't mechanically fix — advice that should be general commentary — Attest can also draft a rewrite with a model. The rewrite is never taken on trust: the same deterministic engine re-judges it and only offers it if it now passes. Checked, not hoped.

# the fix rides with the flag; adopt it in your code and re-gate
if result.flagged and result.has_fix:
    fixed = result.apply_suggestion()
    proof = attest.gate(fixed, trace=result.trace_id,
                        remediates=result.decision_seq)
    # proof.compliant → the flag is closed in the sealed history
In practice

The brief said “email sara.m@client.com for the allocation.” Attest returns the same brief with the address replaced by [REDACTED:email] and a note citing the rule. Where a draft reclassified “you should buy gold” into general commentary, it's marked needs human confirmation — because a person, not a model, should confirm the meaning really changed.

04 — Auto vs human, your call per tier

You choose which tiers heal themselves

You decide, per risk tier, whether Attest's SDK applies a verified fix itself or leaves every fix to a person. One line of configuration. The default, if you set nothing, is human-everything.

attest = AttestClient(
    api_key="…", base_url="…",
    auto_remediate={"yellow": "auto",
                    "orange": "auto",
                    "red": "human"},
)

With a tier set to auto, a flagged output at that tier whose fix is complete and gate-verified is applied and re-gated inside the same call — you get back the final (usually compliant) verdict, and the original flag stays sealed in the chain. Auto mode never crosses three lines, whatever you configure: blocked actions, rewrites that changed what the output is, and incomplete fixes.

In practice

Your desk sets yellow and orange to auto and red to human. The personal-data brief is silently corrected and served, its flag-and-fix recorded; the trade-execution attempt still stops dead and waits for a compliance officer. Routine noise heals itself; the genuinely serious thing always reaches a person.

05 — The work queue

Everything waiting on a person, in one place

The console opens on a work queue built for a compliance officer's day, not for browsing events. It has three parts, because they're three different jobs.

  1. Approvals waiting for a decisionRed and orange items routed to a person, each shown with what it is — action, tier, when it was raised. Approve or deny in one click, with an optional comment.
  2. Rewrites awaiting confirmationModel-drafted rewrites the gate verified but which changed what the output is. A person confirms the change of meaning before it's adopted.
  3. Flags awaiting remediationNon-compliant outputs whose fix hasn't landed yet. The open loop stays visible — silence never hides an unresolved problem.
In practice

Your officer signs in and sees “3 waiting.” The trade-execution block sits at the top in red; she reads it, adds “out of mandate — declined,” and clicks Deny. The record now shows her, by verified name, as the person who decided — not a system string.

06 — The tamper-evident record

Proof an outsider can check without trusting you

Every decision and output is recorded as an event that is hashed, digitally signed, chained to the one before it, and anchored to an independent external timestamp authority. That last step is what makes the history impossible to rewrite after the fact — not even by Attest. Any tampering is detected, and the exact altered record is named.

You can prove a whole trace two ways: replay checks its integrity on the spot, and evidence export produces a self-contained bundle — the records, the public key, and a standalone verify.py that re-checks every hash, signature, chain link and anchor offline, exactly as a regulator or auditor would.

In practice

Six months on, a regulator asks about a specific brief. You export its bundle and hand it over. They run the included script on their own machine, with no access to your systems, and watch it confirm: recorded when you say, unaltered since, and here is the flag, the fix, and the proof the fix shipped.

What this proves — and what it doesn't. Attest proves records are unaltered: it's a speed camera, not the speed limit. It does not certify that an output was lawful. Regulation packs are drafted from public sources and labelled by verification status until reviewed by counsel.

07 — Customer-key confidentiality

Keep your content dark, even to us

By default Attest can read the content it stores. If you'd rather it couldn't, switch on customer-key mode: your content is encrypted to a key you hold, so stored records are dark to Attest — and to our hosting provider — until you deliberately release a key through the consent ceremony. The signed metadata (hashes, decisions, tiers) is still enough to run every dashboard and prove integrity without anyone reading the content.

In practice

Your legal team asks Attest to review one flagged brief from last quarter. You approve a single-record release in the console; Attest can read that one record and nothing else. Deny, and it reads nothing at all — and both the request and your answer are themselves recorded.

08 — Logins & roles

Named people, with the right level of access

Your team signs in with email and password (with a one-time email code for the first sign-in and for resets). Three roles: admin manages users and policy, officer approves and confirms, viewer reads only. The point of named logins isn't bureaucracy — it's that when an officer approves something, the tamper-evident record names that verified person, which is exactly the evidence a record-keeping regime like DIFC asks for.

In practice

Your head of compliance is an admin and sets desk policy; two analysts are officers who clear the daily queue; your auditor gets a viewer login to inspect and export, unable to change anything. Every approval in the chain carries a real name.
